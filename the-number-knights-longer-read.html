The Number Knights and the Missing Crystal

Chapter 1The Morning the Numbers Went Wrong

In the kingdom of Numeria, numbers were everywhere.

They were painted on the doors and stitched onto the flags. They hung in the trees like apples.

High on the tallest tower sat the Great Maths Crystal.

As long as it shone, every number in Numeria made sense.

Then, one morning, the crystal was gone.

By breakfast, the numbers had gone wobbly. The town clock struck banana. The baker’s sign said “3 buns for 0 pennies”, and there was a very long queue.

On the school wall, 2 + 2 said it was 5, then 9, then a small picture of a duck.

King Zero looked down from his window.

He was round and calm, and very good at keeping things in their place.

“This will not do,” he said. “Send for the Number Knights!”

There were four of them.

Sir Plus was big and jolly, and he always had room for one more.

Lady Minus was tidy and quick. She took away whatever wasn’t needed, like clutter, and worries, and once, by accident, the King’s lunch.

Sir Times did everything in lots. Lots of talking.

Lots of galloping. His proper name was Sir Multiply, but nobody had time to say it.

Lady Share made sure everyone got the same.

Her proper name was Lady Divide, but she liked Share better. It sounded kinder.

And behind them, on a very small pony, came Equals.

Equals was not a knight. Not yet. She was a squire, which meant she carried things. She carried the snack bag. And she carried a pair of golden scales, because her job was to check that both sides of every sum were the same.

The knights called this “the boring bit”.

“Knights,” said King Zero, “find the crystal, before the whole kingdom forgets how to count.”

“We will!” shouted the four knights.

“Me too,” said Equals. But she said it so quietly that nobody heard.

Chapter 2The Broken Bridge

The road out of Numeria crossed a deep, fast river.

The bridge needed 20 planks. Somebody had counted them out that morning, when the numbers were already wobbly.

There were only 13.

“Stand back,” said Sir Plus. “This is a job for adding. We have 13 planks and we need 20. So how many more?”

Your turn. Can you work it out before Sir Plus does?

He counted on his big fingers.

“14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20. Seven more!”

He chopped 7 planks and banged them into place.

Equals took out her little brass weights. She put 13 and 7 on one side of her scales, and 20 on the other.

The scales sat perfectly level.

“13 and 7 is the same as 20,” she said.

But the knights were already halfway across the bridge.

Chapter 3The Dragon’s Pizza

On the far side of the river was a cave, and in the cave lived the Fraction Dragon and his baby brother.

The Fraction Dragon was not a scary dragon. He was a hungry one. His smoke smelled of burnt cheese.

“Have you seen the Great Maths Crystal?” asked Lady Share.

“I might have,” said the dragon. “Solve my riddle first. This morning we had one whole pizza. I ate half of it. Then my baby brother ate half of what was left. How much pizza is left for supper?”

“None!” said Equals. It just popped out.

“Not quite,” said the dragon, and a smoke ring puffed out of his nose.

Equals went as red as a tomato. She hid behind the snack bag.

Lady Share drew a circle in the sand with the tip of her sword.

“Here is the pizza,” she said. She drew a line down the middle. “The dragon ate this half. That leaves one half.” She drew a line across the half that was left.

“His brother ate half of the half. So only this piece is left. If you cut the whole pizza into pieces this size, you would get four.”

Your turn. What do we call one of four equal pieces?

“One quarter!” said Lady Share.

“Correct,” said the dragon.

Lady Share bent down beside the snack bag.

“You knew that some of the pizza had been eaten,” she said softly. “That was a good start. A wrong answer is not the end, Equals. It is a clue.”

Equals peeped out. She had never thought of it like that.

“I saw something shiny go by last night,” said the dragon. “Somebody in a pointy hat carried it across the sea.”

Chapter 4The Pirates of the Sea of Sums

By the sea they found a rowing boat and four pirates.

Their captain was called Captain Dot, because of the big white dot on his hat.

“We’ll row you across,” said Captain Dot. “But she’s sitting too low in the water. We’ve got 30 cannonballs on board, and she only floats with 18.”

“Then some must go,” said Lady Minus happily.

“30, take away something, leaves 18.”

Your turn. How many cannonballs have to come out?

“Twelve!” said Lady Minus.

The pirates rolled 12 cannonballs onto the sand, and the boat bobbed up like a cork.

“And rowing is hungry work,” said Captain Dot.

“Five biscuits for each rower.”

“Four rowers, five biscuits each,” said Sir Times, very fast. “Four lots of five. Twenty!”

Equals opened the snack bag and counted out 20 biscuits. She checked both sums on her scales.

They were level both times.

“Thank you, small person,” said Captain Dot.

He was the first one all day to notice her.

Chapter 5The Door That Would Not Open

Across the sea stood the Castle of Shapes.

Its towers were triangles. Its windows were circles.

Its great square door was shut tight.

In front of the door hung a huge pair of stone scales.

In the left pan sat two stones, marked 9 and 6.

In the right pan sat one stone, marked 8, with an empty space beside it.

Next to the scales was a basket of number stones.

Over the door, somebody had carved five words:

MAKE BOTH SIDES THE SAME.

“Easy,” said Sir Plus. “9 and 6 makes 15. The answer is 15!”

He dropped the 15 stone into the right pan.

CRASH. The right side thumped to the ground.

The door did not move.

“Take something away!” said Lady Minus.

“Put in lots!” said Sir Times.

“Share them out!” said Lady Share.

They all grabbed stones at once. The scales swung up and down like a seesaw.

“Excuse me,” said Equals. Nobody heard.

“Excuse me,” she said, a bit louder. Nobody heard.

Her tummy felt wobbly. What if she was wrong again?

Then she remembered. A wrong answer is not the end. It is a clue.

Equals climbed onto the snack bag.

“STOP!”

Four knights stopped, with their arms full of stones.

“It doesn’t say find the answer,” said Equals. “It says make both sides the same. The left side is 9 and 6. That makes 15. So the right side has to make 15 too. It already has 8.”

Your turn. 8 and what makes 15?

“Seven,” said Equals. Her voice shook, but she said it.

She took out the 15 stone and put the 7 stone beside the 8.

The scales creaked. They wobbled. And then they sat perfectly, beautifully level.

Clunk. The great square door swung open.

Chapter 6The Lonely Wizard

Inside, at a table covered in puzzles, sat the Shape Wizard. The Great Maths Crystal glowed in his hands.

“I only borrowed it,” he said. He looked down at his pointy shoes. “Nobody ever comes here to do puzzles with me. I thought that if I had the crystal, the numbers might come and keep me company. I didn’t know yours would go wobbly.”

The knights looked at one another. They had been ready for a fight. They were not ready for this.

Equals stepped forward.

“It isn’t the same on both sides,” she said. “You have all the numbers, and Numeria has none. But you have nobody to play with, and we have lots of people.”

She thought for a moment. “Come back with us. Bring your puzzles. Then both sides are fair.”

The Wizard blinked. “You would want me to?”

“We have biscuits,” said Equals. “Well. We did.”

So the crystal went home to its tower, and the Wizard came too.

The numbers stopped wobbling at once.

The clock struck six. 2 + 2 was 4 again. Buns cost one penny each, which pleased the baker and nobody else.

That evening, in front of the whole kingdom, King Zero called Equals forward and touched his sword gently to her shoulder.

“Arise, Lady Equals, Knight of Numeria,” he said.

“Plus, Minus, Times and Share can do wonderful things. But without you, a sum is only a question.”

And every Friday after that, the Shape Wizard came to the castle for Puzzle Day. He lost quite often.

He didn’t mind a bit.

So if a sum ever makes your tummy feel wobbly, remember what the five Number Knights say.

Maths is not a monster. It is a puzzle.

A wrong answer is not the end. It is a clue.

And puzzles are best when we solve them together.

The End
